Layer Height, Infill and Walls: What Each Setting Really Does

Slicers offer hundreds of settings, but three of them decide most of what a print looks like, how strong it is and how long it takes: layer height, infill and wall count. Understanding them makes it much easier to choose sensible defaults, to describe your tested settings in a listing and to estimate time and material. This guide explains each one in plain language, with typical ranges for a 0.4 mm nozzle.

Layer height

Layer height is the thickness of each printed layer. Thinner layers make smoother curves and finer detail, but they multiply the number of layers and the printing time.

Layer heightEffectGood for
0.08 to 0.12 mmVery smooth, slowSmall detailed figures
0.16 to 0.20 mmBalanced quality and speedMost models
0.24 to 0.32 mmVisible layers, fastLarge functional parts and prototypes

A common rule is to keep the layer height between 20 and 80 percent of the nozzle diameter. For a 0.4 mm nozzle that means roughly 0.08 to 0.32 mm. Halving the layer height roughly doubles the number of layers, and time grows accordingly, though not always exactly twofold because of travel moves and cooling.

Walls (perimeters)

Walls are the outlines printed around each layer. They give a part its shape, its surface and most of its strength. With a 0.4 mm nozzle, two walls produce about 0.8 mm of shell, and three walls about 1.2 mm. For many parts, adding walls makes them stronger more efficiently than increasing infill, because the load is carried by the outside of the object.

  • Two walls: fine for decorative models.
  • Three to four walls: good for parts that will be handled, screwed or loaded.
  • More walls: useful for threads, hooks and parts that bend.

Top and bottom layers

These are the solid skins that close the model. Their count should give a total thickness of about 0.8 to 1.2 mm. At 0.2 mm layers, that means four to six layers. Too few top layers lead to gaps or pillowing over sparse infill.

Infill

Infill is the pattern printed inside the walls. Its density is given as a percentage of the interior volume. It supports the top layers and adds stiffness, but its main effect is on time and material, not on strength.

InfillUse
0 to 10 percentDisplay models, figures that carry no load
15 to 25 percentGeneral purpose, the usual default
30 to 50 percentFunctional parts under load
60 percent and aboveRarely worth the cost; add walls instead

The pattern matters too. Gyroid and cubic patterns are strong in several directions and print reasonably fast, grid and lines are quick and simple, and lightning infill saves material under flat top surfaces for decorative objects.

How the settings work together

A figure printed at 0.12 mm layers, two walls and 10 percent infill is slow but looks excellent. A bracket printed at 0.24 mm layers, four walls and 25 percent infill is fast and strong. Neither is better; they serve different purposes. When you publish a model, share the settings you tested so buyers can start from a result that worked.

Effects on cost and time

  • Layer height mostly changes time.
  • Infill changes material and time.
  • Walls change material, time and strength.

You can combine this with the weight-estimating method to quote a price. Higher infill and more walls move the fill factor in your estimate upward.

Common mistakes

  • Using 80 percent infill to make a part strong instead of adding walls.
  • Choosing very thin layers for a large, plain object where nobody sees the benefit.
  • Setting too few top layers on a model with a large flat top.
  • Not adjusting settings for different materials; follow the filament maker's recommended ranges.

A simple starting profile

  1. 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers.
  2. Three walls, five top and bottom layers.
  3. 15 percent gyroid infill.
  4. Print one small test, then adjust one setting at a time.

Changing one setting at a time lets you see what each does for your printer, and your notes become the most useful part of any listing.
