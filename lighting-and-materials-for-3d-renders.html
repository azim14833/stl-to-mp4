Lighting, Colour and Material Choices for 3D Model Renders

Two renders of the same model can look completely different, depending only on light, colour and material. A well-lit preview makes a print look solid, detailed and desirable. A badly lit one makes a good design look flat or cheap. This guide explains the few decisions that matter and how to match your preview to what a buyer will really hold.

Start with the purpose of the picture

A listing image has to do three jobs: show the overall shape, show the detail, and show what the finished object will look like. Lighting serves the first two. Material and colour serve the third. Decide which job a particular image does before you adjust anything.

Lighting basics: key, fill and rim

  • Key light: the main light, placed to one side and slightly above the camera, about 30 to 45 degrees from the line of sight. It creates the shadows that show shape.
  • Fill light: a weaker light from the opposite side, at roughly a quarter to half of the key's strength, so shadow areas keep some detail.
  • Rim light: a light from behind that outlines the silhouette and separates dark models from the background.

Flat lighting from the camera direction removes shadows and hides surface detail. Very hard light makes black holes in the shadows. Soft, large light sources are more forgiving, and they suit the rounded forms of most printed figures.

Shadows and grounding

A soft contact shadow under the model tells the eye that the object is sitting on a surface. Without it, a model looks like it is floating. Keep the shadow gentle; a strong, hard shadow draws attention to the floor instead of the model.

Matte, glossy and metal

LookMatchesWatch out for
Matte plasticStandard PLA, PETG and ABS printsCan look flat if lit from the front

ClayShowing shape without distracting colourNot representative of a coloured print
MetalMetal-filled filaments, polished partsReflects the environment, so a plain scene looks dull

Choose the finish that resembles your real product. If you print in matte PLA, a mirror-like render promises more than you deliver.

Choosing the colour

Pick a colour with enough tonal contrast to show relief. Mid-tones such as medium grey, muted blue or terracotta show shadows and highlights well. Very dark models lose their form in shadow, and very light models turn white when overexposed. When you sell finished prints, render in the filament colour you actually stock. Mention if other colours are available.

Backgrounds

A neutral background lets the model carry the picture. The best choices are plain grey gradients and soft studio floors. Avoid backgrounds that share the colour of the model, patterned photos that fight for attention, and anything that makes thin parts hard to see. For multi-view sets, use the same background in every image.

Showing detail

  • Use a three-quarter view from slightly above for most detail.
  • Use a wireframe image to prove that a model has real geometry and not just smooth shading.
  • For engraved text, light from a low angle so the edges catch shadow.
  • For layer lines, keep the render honest and add a real photo for texture.

Be honest about the colour

Screens vary. A render can be only a guide to a real print's colour, so state the filament brand and colour name in the listing. If a model is meant to be painted, say so. Buyers who know what to expect leave better reviews.

A quick setup you can reuse

  1. Neutral grey background with a soft shadow.
  2. Matte material in a mid-tone colour.
  3. Camera 20 to 30 degrees above the horizon.
  4. Key light front-left, weaker fill front-right, a subtle rim from behind.
  5. The same settings for every model in the shop.

Once you have a setup you like, save it and reuse it. Consistency does more for a shop than any single clever image.
