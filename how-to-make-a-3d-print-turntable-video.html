How to Make a Turntable Video of a 3D Model

A short rotating video shows the shape of a model far better than a single still. Buyers can see the back, the overhangs and the proportions without guessing. Whether the clip looks professional or amateur depends on a handful of choices. This guide covers each one and gives concrete numbers you can copy.

1. Prepare the model first

Turntable footage punishes sloppy preparation. Rotate the model so its base is flat and its most interesting side faces the camera at the start. If the file was exported with the wrong up axis, correct that first; otherwise the model tumbles end over end instead of spinning on its base. Check the scale if you plan to show dimensions. Finally, centre the model so it rotates around its own middle. An off-centre model swings in and out of frame and looks wobbly.

2. Choose a calm background

The model should be the hero. A soft grey gradient, a plain studio floor or a gentle contact shadow works better than a busy scene. Mind the contrast: a white model on a white background disappears, and a black model on a dark scene loses all its detail. A mid-tone background, such as a light or medium grey, suits most colours. If you sell on several platforms, a transparent background lets you reuse the same render on different banners, though video files do not normally keep transparency, so use a solid colour for MP4.

3. Light the model so the surface reads

Light from one side and slightly above, then soften the shadow side with a weaker fill light. Flat, frontal lighting hides layer lines, engraved detail and curvature. A very strong single light creates black areas where detail is lost. If a scene offers a soft shadow option, use it; a model that appears to sit on a surface feels real and printable.

4. Pick a camera angle

An elevation of about 20 to 30 degrees feels natural, like looking down at a model on a desk. Straight-on side views flatten the shape, and steep top-down views hide the silhouette. Frame the model so it keeps a margin of 10 to 15 percent of the frame on every side throughout the whole rotation. Tall thin models and wide flat models need different framing: check the widest point of the rotation, not just the front view.

5. Set the duration and make it loop

One full rotation in 6 to 10 seconds feels smooth. Faster spins look nervous and hide detail; slower ones look sleepy and enlarge the file. Rotate exactly 360 degrees at constant speed. Do not ease in and out, because a slowdown at the end creates a visible hitch when the clip repeats. For a seamless loop, the last frame should not repeat the first: at 30 fps, an 8-second turn is 240 frames, drawn at 0, 1.5, 3 and so on up to 358.5 degrees.

7. Check the result on a phone

Most buyers will watch on a phone. Watch your export there and look for flickering thin parts, shadows that jump, banding in gradients, and clipped edges. If the file is larger than the platform's upload limit, lower the bitrate before you lower the resolution. If thin parts shimmer, try a slightly slower rotation or a higher bitrate.

Common mistakes

  • Letting the model touch the frame edge at its widest point.
  • A rotation that does not finish exactly at 360 degrees, which creates a jump on replay.
  • Posting a 40 MB video where 8 MB would look identical on a phone.
  • Using a background colour close to the filament colour you are selling.

Pair the video with still images from fixed angles so buyers who cannot play video still see every side.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a turntable video be?

One revolution of 6 to 10 seconds is enough. Platforms loop short clips, and viewers rarely watch a model spin for longer than that.

Should I add music?

Not for marketplaces, which often mute previews. For social posts you may add audio in a separate editor, but only use music you have the right to use.

Why does my video look fine on my PC and blocky on a phone?

Phones recompress uploads and display them on small screens with different scaling. Raise the bitrate slightly, avoid very fine patterns in the background, and check the clip after it has been uploaded, not only before.

What if my model is very long or very flat?

A long model swings wide on a turntable, so frame it by its longest dimension. For very flat objects, raise the camera to 35 to 45 degrees so the top detail is visible.

Do I need a transparent background for video?

No. MP4 does not normally keep transparency. Use a solid background colour for video and a transparent PNG for stills if you want to place the model on your own artwork.
