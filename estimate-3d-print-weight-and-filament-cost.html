How to Estimate 3D Print Weight and Filament Cost from an STL

Before printing or quoting a price, it helps to know roughly how much material a model will use. You do not need to slice it first: a model's volume, a material density and an infill percentage give a surprisingly good estimate.

Step 1: get the volume

A closed STL has a measurable volume, usually reported in cubic centimetres. This only works if the unit is correct, so confirm the size first. A model that is 10 cm in every direction has 1000 cm3 as a solid cube; most real models are far less because of curves and hollow shapes.

Step 2: multiply by density

Typical filament densities are close to these values:

  • PLA: about 1.24 g/cm3
  • PETG: about 1.27 g/cm3
  • ABS: about 1.04 g/cm3
  • TPU: about 1.2 g/cm3

Check the data sheet of your filament, because brands vary and additives change the value. A fully solid 100 cm3 PLA object would weigh about 124 grams.

Step 3: account for infill and walls

Real prints are not solid. They consist of outer walls, top and bottom layers and a sparse interior pattern. A rough rule is to count about 20 to 40 percent of the solid weight for a typical 15 to 20 percent infill print with two or three walls. Small models with thin parts are mostly walls, so they come closer to the solid weight; large chunky models gain more from low infill.

Step 4: estimate the cost

Divide the price of a spool by its weight to get the price per gram. If a 1 kg spool costs 20 currency units, each gram costs 0.02. A 60 gram print therefore uses 1.20 units of filament. Add electricity, failed prints, support material and your time if you sell prints.

Use the estimate wisely

This method is for planning and quoting, not exact accounting. Supports, brims, purge towers and settings change the real result, so treat the number as a starting point and compare it with your slicer's figure after your first few prints. Over time you will learn your own correction factor.
