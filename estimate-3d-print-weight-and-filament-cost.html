How to Estimate 3D Print Weight and Filament Cost from an STL

Before you print, or quote a price to a customer, it helps to know roughly how much material a model will use. You do not have to slice it first. A model's volume, the density of the filament and a sensible allowance for infill give an estimate that is usually close enough for planning. This guide walks through the method with numbers, then shows where the estimate goes wrong.

Step 1: get the volume

A closed (watertight) mesh encloses a measurable volume, normally reported in cubic centimetres. Viewers calculate it by summing the signed volumes of tiny tetrahedra formed by each triangle and the origin, which is why it only works if the mesh has no holes. The result depends entirely on the unit, so fix the scale first. A 10 cm cube has 1000 cm³ of volume; most real models fill only a fraction of their bounding box because of curves, hollows and thin parts.

Step 2: multiply by density

MaterialTypical density100 cm³ solid weighs
PLAabout 1.24 g/cm³124 g
PETGabout 1.27 g/cm³127 g
ABSabout 1.04 g/cm³104 g
ASAabout 1.07 g/cm³107 g
TPUabout 1.2 g/cm³120 g

These are typical values. Brands differ and additives such as carbon fibre, wood or metal powder change the number, so check the data sheet of your spool when accuracy matters.

Step 3: allow for infill and walls

A real print is not solid. It consists of outer walls, solid top and bottom layers and a sparse interior pattern. How much of the solid weight you actually use depends on the shape:

  • Thin or small parts are mostly walls and skins, so they land at 60 to 100 percent of the solid weight.
  • Medium compact parts with 15 to 20 percent infill and two or three walls usually use 30 to 50 percent of the solid weight.
  • Large chunky parts benefit most from low infill and can fall to 20 to 35 percent.

These bands are rules of thumb, not guarantees. They are the right starting point for a first quote; you will refine them with your own prints.

A worked example

A figurine measures 45 cm³ in the viewer and you will print it in PLA.

  1. Solid weight: 45 × 1.24 = 55.8 g.
  2. Medium compact part with 15 percent infill and three walls: assume about 40 percent, so 55.8 × 0.40 = about 22 g.
  3. Add 10 to 15 percent for supports, skirt and a small safety margin: about 25 g.
  4. A 1 kg spool costs 20 currency units, so one gram costs 0.02. Material cost: 25 × 0.02 = 0.50.

Step 4: remember the other costs

Filament is rarely the whole price. If you sell prints, also account for:

  • Electricity. A hobby printer averages somewhere near 100 W while printing, though this varies a lot with heated beds and enclosures. An 8-hour print at 100 W is 0.8 kWh. A cheap plug-in power meter tells you your real figure.
  • Failed prints. If one in ten prints fails, add about ten percent to the price of the others.
  • Machine wear. Nozzles, belts and build plates are consumables.
  • Your time for post-processing, packing and messages.

Where the estimate goes wrong

  • Wrong unit. A model that is 25 times too small will report a volume about 15,000 times too small. Always check the size first.
  • Open or broken meshes. Holes make the volume unreliable. Repair the mesh before trusting it.
  • Supports and brims. Overhang-heavy models can use 20 to 50 percent extra material in supports.
  • Purge towers and colour changes. Multi-colour prints waste filament on every change.
  • Hollow models. If a model is already hollow, the walls dominate and the infill allowance is far too low.

Calibrate against your slicer

For your first ten prints, write down the estimate and the slicer's figure side by side. After a few prints the ratio between them becomes stable for your printer and settings, and you can multiply future estimates by that correction factor. At that point you can quote prices from a viewer in seconds and be right within a few grams.

A quick formula you can reuse

Put the whole method in one line: cost = volume (cm³) × density (g/cm³) × fill factor × 1.1 × price per gram. The fill factor is between 0.3 and 1.0 depending on how thin and hollow the part is, and 1.1 adds a ten percent margin for supports and purge. For a spreadsheet, keep volume, density and price per gram in separate cells and change only the fill factor per model type.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is a viewer's weight estimate?

Viewers usually report the weight of a solid object. Compare that number with your fill factor and you will land within 10 to 20 percent for most hobby prints.

Does layer height change the weight?

Only slightly. It changes time and surface quality far more than material use, although thicker layers can change how infill and skins are laid down.

How do I price a print for someone else?

Add material, electricity and a failure allowance, then add your time. Many sellers multiply the material cost by a fixed factor, for example three, and adjust for complexity. Whatever you choose, apply it the same way every time.
